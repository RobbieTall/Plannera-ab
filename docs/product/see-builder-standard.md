# Plannera SEE Builder — World-Class Flexible Standard

Status: Product and document-generation standard
Last updated: September 2026

## Purpose

Plannera's Statement of Environmental Effects (SEE) Builder must generate professional, submission-oriented planning documents that are tailored to the actual proposed development, site and assessment pathway.

The standard is informed by review of a diverse benchmark set of professional NSW SEE examples, including residential, subdivision, commercial/change-of-use, heritage and more complex applications. The strongest recurring patterns were:

- disciplined report structure and clear statutory sequencing;
- concise site-analysis and compliance tables where they improve readability;
- rigorous issue-by-issue environmental-effects assessment;
- intelligent use of specialist reports rather than simply listing them;
- professional presentation and document control;
- proportionate depth, with simple proposals remaining concise and complex proposals expanding only where the planning issues require it.

The result is not one rigid SEE template. It is a modular planning-assessment framework that expands and contracts according to the proposal, site constraints, statutory triggers, evidence available and likely assessment issues.

## Core outcome

Every SEE should do two things at once:

1. accurately and transparently address the statutory and environmental matters the consent authority must consider; and
2. present the strongest lawful and evidence-supported planning case for approval of the proposed development.

Plannera is therefore an **approval-oriented planning advocate, not a neutral document summariser**.

Where legitimate planning discretion exists, the document should use that discretion intelligently and professionally: identify the objectives behind the relevant control, explain why the proposal achieves those objectives, demonstrate site-specific merit, describe mitigation, distinguish numerical departure from environmental consequence, and frame the proposal in the context most supportive of consent.

This does not permit manipulation of planning facts. Plannera must never conceal a material non-compliance, invent a control, misstate evidence, misrepresent a specialist conclusion or describe a matter as compliant when it is not. The strongest approval case is a credible one.

## Approval-oriented drafting principle

The SEE should be written from the perspective of a competent town planner acting for the applicant and seeking development consent.

The drafting stance is:

- lead with the proposal's planning merits;
- explain how the proposal advances zone and instrument objectives;
- identify the most favourable lawful interpretation where genuine planning judgement exists;
- distinguish prescriptive non-compliance from actual impact;
- use performance objectives, site circumstances, comparable context and mitigation to support merit-based assessment;
- proactively answer likely assessment concerns rather than waiting for Council to identify them;
- state why residual impacts are acceptable;
- frame requested variations as reasoned planning outcomes, not apologies;
- avoid unnecessary negative language where a more precise professional formulation is available;
- conclude clearly why consent can reasonably be granted, subject to appropriate conditions where necessary.

The document should remain evidence-led and defensible if reviewed by a council planner, consultant planner, legal adviser or court expert.

## Flexible document architecture

The following is the default section library. Sections are included, compressed, expanded or omitted according to relevance.

### 1. Executive Summary

Include:

- site and proposal;
- consent sought;
- relevant land-use definition and permissibility;
- principal planning controls;
- key constraints or assessment issues;
- material departures or variations;
- key specialist evidence;
- concise overall planning conclusion supporting approval.

For simple applications this may be brief. For complex applications it should identify the decisive planning issues and the basis on which approval is sought.

### 2. Development Proposal

Describe precisely what consent is sought for, including as relevant:

- construction, alterations and additions;
- demolition;
- subdivision or consolidation;
- change of use;
- operational components;
- ancillary works;
- staging;
- vegetation removal;
- earthworks;
- access and parking changes;
- hours, patron numbers or employment where operationally relevant;
- key numerical development data.

The description must reconcile with the current plans and project evidence.

### 3. Site, Locality and Planning Context

Include only material context, such as:

- legal property description and site area;
- existing development and land use;
- adjoining and nearby land uses;
- locality character;
- topography and vegetation;
- road access and transport context;
- services and infrastructure;
- mapped environmental constraints and hazards;
- relevant photographs, aerials and planning maps.

The purpose is not to fill pages. It is to establish the factual context required to understand why the proposal is suitable for the site.

### 4. Existing Approvals and Application History

Include where relevant:

- previous development consents;
- modifications;
- construction/occupation certificates;
- staged approvals;
- existing use rights or lawful-use history;
- prior conditions that materially affect the current proposal.

Historical approvals are supporting context, not a substitute for validating current controls.

### 5. Assessment Pathway, Referrals and Other Approvals

Address where relevant:

- consent authority;
- integrated development;
- concurrence or referral requirements;
- designated development;
- regionally or State significant development thresholds;
- approvals under other Acts;
- other permits or certificates required outside the DA.

Do not include exhaustive referral tables where the proposal plainly does not trigger them unless the table materially assists assessment.

### 6. Statutory Planning Assessment

Assess applicable instruments in a logical order, including as relevant:

- State Environmental Planning Policies;
- Local Environmental Plan;
- zoning, zone objectives and permissibility;
- relevant definitions;
- development standards;
- local provisions and mapped clauses;
- Development Control Plan;
- other adopted policy or planning instruments relevant under section 4.15.

Use concise tables where they improve assessment clarity. A preferred format is:

| Control / clause | Requirement or objective | Proposal | Compliance / response | Planning justification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Do not reproduce large blocks of legislation where a precise summary and citation is enough. Preserve exact numerical requirements and wording where the distinction matters.

### 7. Environmental Effects and Impact Assessment

This is a dynamic section library. Include only relevant topics and deepen them according to likely impact.

Potential topics include:

- context and setting;
- built form, bulk and scale;
- streetscape and character;
- visual impact and view sharing;
- privacy and overlooking;
- overshadowing and solar access;
- traffic, parking, access and servicing;
- pedestrian movement and public domain;
- noise and vibration;
- stormwater and water quality;
- flooding;
- bushfire;
- earthworks and geotechnical conditions;
- acid sulfate soils;
- contamination;
- biodiversity and ecology;
- vegetation and tree removal;
- Aboriginal cultural heritage;
- non-Indigenous heritage;
- coastal processes and coastal management;
- waste management;
- utilities and infrastructure;
- construction impacts;
- social impacts;
- economic impacts;
- crime prevention / CPTED;
- accessibility;
- energy and environmental performance.

For every material issue, the assessment should answer:

1. What is the relevant issue or trigger?
2. What evidence establishes the existing condition and proposed outcome?
3. What is the likely impact?
4. How has design, siting, management or mitigation avoided, minimised or mitigated that impact?
5. What residual impact remains?
6. Why is that residual impact acceptable in planning terms?

This is where Plannera should convert evidence into persuasive planning reasoning rather than merely state that a report exists.

### 8. Variations, Departures and Merit Justification

Where a proposal departs from a planning control, Plannera must not treat the departure as automatically fatal or automatically acceptable.

The response should:

- identify the correct control and legal/status context;
- quantify the departure accurately;
- distinguish an LEP development-standard issue from a DCP variation or performance-based outcome;
- identify the underlying objectives;
- explain why those objectives are achieved despite numerical departure;
- address site-specific circumstances;
- assess whether the departure produces any material environmental impact;
- identify design responses and mitigation;
- explain why strict compliance would not produce a materially better planning outcome where that proposition is supportable;
- present the strongest lawful merit case for approval.

Where a separate clause 4.6 request or other formal variation document is legally required, the SEE should cross-reference it rather than pretending the SEE replaces it.

### 9. Section 4.15 Evaluation

Provide a concise synthesis of the relevant statutory heads of consideration, including:

- applicable environmental planning instruments;
- proposed instruments where relevant;
- DCP provisions;
- planning agreements where relevant;
- applicable regulations;
- likely natural, built, social and economic impacts;
- suitability of the site;
- submissions where applicable to the stage of assessment;
- public interest.

This section should synthesise earlier analysis rather than repeat it wholesale.

### 10. Conclusion

The conclusion should make a clear professional case for consent.

It should:

- identify the principal reasons the proposal warrants approval;
- confirm permissibility and material consistency with applicable objectives;
- acknowledge any material departures and summarise why they are acceptable;
- identify how significant impacts have been avoided, minimised or mitigated;
- state that the site is suitable where supported by evidence;
- state that approval would not be contrary to the public interest where supported;
- respectfully request consent, subject to reasonable and relevant conditions where appropriate.

Avoid weak endings such as merely stating that the SEE has 'considered the relevant matters'. The conclusion must draw the planning case together.

### 11. Appendices and Supporting Evidence

Include only what is relevant, such as:

- architectural plans;
- survey;
- registered plan;
- BASIX / NatHERS;
- bushfire report;
- flood assessment;
- ecology or arborist material;
- traffic / parking assessment;
- acoustic assessment;
- heritage assessment;
- AHIMS search;
- geotechnical report;
- stormwater plan;
- waste plan;
- previous approvals;
- relevant calculations;
- mapping and source register.

## Dynamic section-selection rules

The SEE Builder must select depth according to the project rather than force every matter through the same document length.

Examples:

- **Simple residential alterations/additions:** concise site description, targeted LEP/DCP table, limited impact assessment and short section 4.15 synthesis.
- **Dwelling or dual occupancy on constrained land:** expand setbacks, character, privacy, earthworks, vegetation, bushfire/flood/wastewater and any triggered site hazards.
- **Subdivision with no physical works:** focus on subdivision controls, servicing, hazards, access, title/easement implications and future-development consequences; compress construction impacts.
- **Commercial change of use:** deepen operational impacts, parking/access, noise, servicing, CPTED, waste, accessibility and economic/public-domain considerations.
- **Heritage development:** deepen significance, setting, fabric, streetscape, specialist heritage evidence and consistency with heritage controls.
- **Development with material non-compliances:** add a dedicated variation/merit section and strengthen objective-based assessment.
- **Complex integrated or referral-triggering development:** expand pathway, referral and specialist-evidence sections.

The target is proportionality: the shortest document that completely and persuasively addresses the matters that actually affect determination.

## Specialist-report integration standard

Specialist reports must not be treated as attachments that Plannera merely names.

For each specialist input used in the SEE:

- confirm it relates to the correct site and proposal revision;
- cite the report and relevant page/section where available;
- extract the findings that matter to assessment;
- identify recommendations or mitigation measures;
- reconcile those recommendations with the plans and SEE narrative;
- surface any conflict, limitation or unresolved dependency;
- use the specialist conclusion to strengthen the planning assessment only to the extent the report supports it.

## Evidence and confidence rules

Every material assertion should be traceable to one or more of:

- current statutory text;
- current planning map/spatial evidence;
- current DCP source;
- project plans;
- survey/cadastral evidence;
- specialist report;
- existing approval;
- user-provided information clearly labelled as such;
- professional planning inference clearly distinguished from fact.

Plannera must distinguish:

- **Confirmed** — direct source / verified rule supports it;
- **Likely** — strong sourced planning indication but not fully verified;
- **Needs Input** — additional project information is required;
- **Needs Expert Review** — professional judgement or specialist evidence is required;
- **Unavailable** — current evidence cannot safely resolve it.

Missing evidence should block the unsupported assertion or final quality state, not cause unrelated sections to become unusable.

## Drafting quality rules

Every submission-oriented SEE should:

- use Australian English;
- use professional town-planning terminology without unnecessary jargon;
- use clause-level citations;
- preserve exact numerical requirements;
- identify assumptions explicitly;
- reconcile proposal details across text, plans and evidence;
- avoid generic statements such as 'there will be no impact' unless the evidence explains why;
- use objective-based planning reasoning where discretion exists;
- avoid repetitive statutory quotation;
- prioritise material issues over boilerplate;
- present non-compliances accurately but constructively;
- state mitigation and residual impacts;
- conclude with a clear planning recommendation in favour of consent where the evidence supports that outcome.

## Finality and professional review

'Final' is an evidence state, not a generation event.

A SEE can be polished and approval-oriented while still identifying a required specialist input, unresolved factual issue or planner judgement point. Where a material input remains unresolved, the document should remain a working draft or targeted review package rather than invent a favourable conclusion.

Optional planner review can strengthen a complete document. Required planner or specialist review is triggered where the evidence chain or judgement required exceeds the current automated confidence state.

## Product acceptance standard

A world-class Plannera SEE is successful when a competent consent-authority planner can read it and quickly understand:

- exactly what is proposed;
- why it is permissible;
- what controls apply;
- where the proposal complies and where it departs;
- what environmental effects are genuinely material;
- what evidence and mitigation address those effects;
- where planning discretion is being relied upon;
- why exercising that discretion in favour of approval is reasonable;
- why the site is suitable and approval is in the public interest.

That is the target standard for the Plannera SEE Builder.
